Overview

Agenda

Spain is in south-western Europe, on the Iberian Peninsula, next to Portugal and France. It has two seas, two groups of islands — the Balearic and the Canary Islands — and the capital, Madrid, is right in the centre. Almost 50 million people live there, in 17 autonomous communities.

The Romans called it Hispania. In 711 Muslims conquered most of the peninsula — that is why we see Arab architecture like the Alhambra. In 1492 the Catholic Kings took Granada, and Columbus sailed from Palos de la Frontera and reached America. In the 16th century Spain was a huge empire. In the 20th century: civil war, then Franco's dictatorship until 1975. Since 1978 Spain is a democracy with a king, and it joined Europe in 1986.

Spaniards eat late: lunch around 2 or 3 pm, dinner after 9. They love sharing tapas, and paella comes from Valencia. There are famous festivals like Las Fallas, the April Fair in Seville or San Fermín in Pamplona. People live outside, on terraces, and football is a passion.

In 2026 the minimum wage is 1,221 euros gross per month, but salaries are paid 14 times a year, with an extra payment in summer and at Christmas — about 17,000 euros a year. The average salary — the latest official figure, from 2024 — is about 29,500 euros. The legal maximum is 40 hours a week, with 30 days of paid holiday.

Housing is the most expensive part. A 50 square metre flat in central Madrid costs about 1,300 euros a month without bills — 91% of the 2026 minimum wage, which is about 1,425 euros a month if you spread the 14 payments over 12 months. Madrid and Barcelona are the most expensive; Valencia, Málaga or Bilbao are around 800 euros, and Seville under 700. And across Spain, rents rose almost 7% in one year.

Transport is much cheaper than housing. A metro ticket in Madrid costs between 1.50 and 2 euros, a 30-day pass for the city 32.70 euros, and for young people from 15 to 25 only 10 euros a month for the whole region.

For longer trips, the train is great. Madrid to Barcelona takes only two and a half hours by high-speed train, and with low-cost companies like Ouigo a ticket costs about 20 euros if you book early. Since January 2026 there is also a single national travel pass: 60 euros a month, or 30 for young people, for commuter trains, regional trains and state-run coaches.

As Europeans we don't need a visa or a work permit, but there are four steps: register as an EU citizen and get an NIE; register your address at the town hall; get a Social Security number; and open a Spanish bank account for your salary.

In a professional context you shake hands; with friends, two kisses. Be on time, even if Spaniards can be a little late. Take time for small talk: relationships matter. Hierarchy matters too: the boss decides. Business lunches are long and you talk business with the coffee. And speaking even a little Spanish makes a very good impression.

Unemployment is about 10% — 10.18% in the third quarter of 2026 — but a record number of people are working: almost 23 million. There are jobs in tourism, IT and healthcare — and for me the most interesting field is energy, especially renewables: it is the best-paid sector, almost 58,000 euros a year. To find a job: SEPE, EURES, InfoJobs or LinkedIn, and apply in Spanish with a Europass CV.

To conclude: Spain is a great country to live and work in — quality of life, an improving job market, cheap transport, and as Europeans we can work there easily. The main problems are housing in Madrid and Barcelona and lower salaries than in France.

04 · Conclusion

Would I live and work in Spain? Yes.

For

  • Quality of life, sun and sea
  • Record employment: 22.9 M jobs
  • Cheap public transport
  • No visa for EU citizens

Against

  • High rents in Madrid and Barcelona
  • Lower salaries than in France
  • You need to speak Spanish
  • Very hot summers
